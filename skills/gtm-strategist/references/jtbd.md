# Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) Framework

## core theory

people don't buy products. they hire them to make progress in their lives. every purchase is driven by a "job" — a struggle or desired outcome that the customer is trying to resolve.

JTBD reframes strategy from "what features should we build?" to "what progress is the customer trying to make?"

## the three job dimensions

every job has three layers. most companies only address the functional one.

**functional job** — the practical task
"i need to send invoices to clients and get paid faster"

**emotional job** — how the person wants to feel
"i want to feel like a professional, not someone chasing payments"

**social job** — how the person wants to be perceived
"i want my clients to see me as organized and legitimate"

→ winning products nail all three. average products only nail the functional.

## job statement format

use this structure to articulate the job clearly:

```
When [situation/context],
I want to [motivation/desired progress],
So I can [expected outcome].
```

**examples:**
- "When I'm onboarding a new client, I want to quickly show them my past work, so I can build credibility without a lengthy pitch."
- "When my team is stuck on a decision, I want to see the data behind each option, so I can break the deadlock and move forward."
- "When I'm about to launch a campaign, I want to know what my competitors just did, so I can differentiate my positioning."

→ good job statements are situation-specific, not generic. "I want to save time" is too vague. "When I'm reconciling monthly expenses across 4 tools, I want one view" is a real job.

## the four forces of switching

when a customer switches from an existing solution to a new one, four forces are at play. map these to understand adoption dynamics.

```
PUSH → frustration with current solution
       "our current tool crashes every time we have more than 50 users"

PULL → attraction of new solution
       "this new tool handles 10K concurrent users and has a free tier"

ANXIETY → fear of switching
          "what if the migration breaks our data? what if the team hates it?"

HABIT → comfort with the status quo
        "we've used this for 3 years. everyone knows the workarounds."
```

→ **push + pull must exceed anxiety + habit** for switching to happen.
→ this is why great products still fail at adoption — they focus on pull but ignore anxiety.

## switch interview methodology

the best way to uncover jobs is to interview people who recently switched solutions (bought your product, churned, or switched to a competitor).

### the timeline

map the customer's journey backward from the moment of purchase:

1. **first thought** — when did they first realize the old way wasn't working?
2. **passive looking** — when did they start noticing alternatives?
3. **active looking** — when did they deliberately search for solutions?
4. **deciding** — what made them pick this specific solution?
5. **consuming** — first use. did it deliver on the promise?
6. **satisfaction** — ongoing. are they still "hired" or looking again?

### interview questions

use these in customer conversations or adapt for surveys:

**uncovering the job:**
1. "tell me about the last time you [did the thing the product does]. walk me through it."
2. "what were you trying to accomplish? what would 'done' look like?"
3. "what was frustrating about how you were doing it before?"
4. "when did you first realize the old way wasn't cutting it anymore?"

**mapping the four forces:**
5. "what finally pushed you to look for something new?" (push)
6. "what caught your attention about [product]?" (pull)
7. "what almost stopped you from switching?" (anxiety)
8. "what did you have to give up or change?" (habit)

**understanding the emotional/social layer:**
9. "how did it make you feel when [the old way failed]?"
10. "what did you tell your team/boss when you recommended [product]?"
11. "if [product] disappeared tomorrow, what would you miss most?"

**testing the job statement:**
12. "if I said the reason you hired [product] is to [job statement], does that feel right? what would you change?"

## applying JTBD to GTM strategy

### positioning
rewrite positioning around the job, not features.

**before (feature-based):** "AI-powered project management with Gantt charts, time tracking, and resource allocation"
**after (job-based):** "When your team is juggling 5 projects and nothing's on track, [Product] gives you one view of what's behind, who's blocked, and what to fix first."

### ICP definition
define your ICP by the job they share, not demographics.

**weak ICP:** "Series A SaaS companies, 20-50 employees, $1-5M ARR"
**strong ICP:** "engineering leaders who just hired their 3rd team and realized their ad-hoc project tracking doesn't scale — and their CEO is starting to ask 'what's the status on X?'"

### competitive differentiation
competitors aren't just other products — they're every way the customer currently gets the job done. including spreadsheets, manual processes, and "just not doing it."

### messaging hierarchy
lead with the job, follow with proof, end with features:
1. "struggling with [situation]?" — name the job
2. "[outcome] in [timeframe]" — promise the progress
3. "here's how: [feature set]" — explain the mechanism

## when NOT to use JTBD

- when the job is already well-understood by the market (commodity categories)
- when the strategic question is about channels/distribution, not positioning
- when the product is so early that there are no customers to interview yet (use hypotheses instead, but validate fast)

## diagnostic questions

before applying JTBD, ask:
- can the client articulate who their customer is and why they buy?
- is the market crowded with similar-sounding products?
- are they selling features and getting commoditized?
- have they talked to customers who recently switched (to them or away)?

if yes to 2+ → JTBD is the right lens.
