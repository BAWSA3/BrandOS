# Blue Ocean Strategy Framework

based on W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne's Blue Ocean Strategy and Blue Ocean Shift.

## core thesis

most companies compete in "red oceans" — crowded markets where everyone fights over the same customers with incrementally better features. margins erode, differentiation disappears, and growth stalls.

blue ocean strategy is about creating uncontested market space — making the competition irrelevant by redefining what the market values.

## red ocean vs. blue ocean diagnostic

before applying this framework, diagnose which ocean the client is swimming in.

**red ocean signals:**
- competitors all describe themselves the same way
- the client's sales team gets asked "how are you different from X?"
- pricing pressure is increasing
- feature parity across top players
- growth is coming from stealing share, not expanding the market
- customer acquisition is getting more expensive over time
- RFPs and comparison matrices define the buying process

**blue ocean signals:**
- the client sees demand they can't serve with existing categories
- there are large groups of "non-customers" who reject the whole category
- the client has a fundamentally different approach but keeps getting compared to incumbents
- customers are over-served on some dimensions and under-served on others

## the value curve

the value curve is the core diagnostic tool. it plots how competing offerings stack up across the factors the industry competes on.

### how to build a value curve

**step 1:** identify the factors the industry competes on (typically 6-12)

example for project management tools:
- number of features
- customization/flexibility
- integrations
- price
- ease of use
- enterprise security
- collaboration features
- reporting/analytics
- mobile experience
- onboarding speed

**step 2:** rate each competitor (including the client) on each factor (1-5 scale)

```
Factor              | Asana | Monday | Jira | Client
--------------------|-------|--------|------|-------
Features            |   4   |   4    |  5   |   2
Customization       |   3   |   4    |  5   |   2
Integrations        |   4   |   4    |  4   |   1
Price (low=cheap)   |   2   |   2    |  3   |   5
Ease of use         |   3   |   3    |  1   |   5
Enterprise security |   4   |   3    |  5   |   1
Collaboration       |   4   |   4    |  3   |   4
Reporting           |   3   |   4    |  5   |   1
Mobile              |   3   |   3    |  2   |   4
Onboarding speed    |   2   |   3    |  1   |   5
```

**step 3:** visualize. if every competitor's line looks the same, it's a red ocean.

→ the strategic insight: find where all competitors are high (opportunity to eliminate/reduce) and where they're all low (opportunity to raise/create).

## the four actions framework

this is the decision-making tool. for each factor on the value curve, ask:

### eliminate
"which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated?"

these are features or capabilities that the industry competes on by default but that your target customer doesn't actually value. eliminating them reduces cost and complexity.

**examples:**
- Cirque du Soleil eliminated animals, star performers, and three-ring format
- Southwest eliminated seat assignments, meals, and hub-and-spoke routing
- Canva eliminated advanced professional tools that 90% of users never touch

### reduce
"which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard?"

these are things that matter but are over-delivered. the industry over-invests because everyone copies each other, not because customers demand it.

**examples:**
- IKEA reduced service (self-serve showroom, self-assembly)
- Netflix reduced content library size but increased quality and personalization
- Stripe reduced feature breadth but simplified the core payment flow

### raise
"which factors should be raised well above the industry standard?"

these are areas where the industry underdelivers — often because the incumbents weren't built for the buyer you're targeting.

**examples:**
- Tesla raised performance and design well above the "eco-car" standard
- Airbnb raised local/authentic experience above hotel standardization
- Notion raised flexibility/customization above rigid SaaS tools

### create
"which factors should be created that the industry has never offered?"

entirely new dimensions of value that didn't exist in the competitive set.

**examples:**
- Peloton created "at-home competitive cycling community"
- Slack created "searchable team communication history"
- Dollar Shave Club created "subscription convenience + brand humor"

### the ERRC grid

summarize your four actions in a grid:

```
┌─────────────────────┬─────────────────────┐
│      ELIMINATE       │       REDUCE        │
│                      │                     │
│ - [factor 1]        │ - [factor 1]        │
│ - [factor 2]        │ - [factor 2]        │
│                      │                     │
├─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┤
│       RAISE          │       CREATE        │
│                      │                     │
│ - [factor 1]        │ - [factor 1]        │
│ - [factor 2]        │ - [factor 2]        │
│                      │                     │
└─────────────────────┴─────────────────────┘
```

→ a good ERRC grid tells a coherent story. the eliminations and reductions fund the raises and creates. it's not a wish list — it's a trade-off map.

## the six paths framework

when the client is stuck in a red ocean and can't see alternatives, use the six paths to systematically find blue ocean opportunities.

### path 1: look across alternative industries
customers often choose between your industry and a completely different one. what can you learn from the alternative?

"people don't choose between Uber and Lyft. they choose between Uber and 'I'll just drive myself.' what does that tell us?"

### path 2: look across strategic groups within the industry
most industries have tiers (budget vs. premium, SMB vs. enterprise). what if you blended elements from different tiers?

"what if you gave enterprise-level security with startup-level simplicity?"

### path 3: look across the chain of buyers
the user, the purchaser, and the influencer are often different people. what if you targeted a different one?

"everyone sells to the CTO. what if the actual user (the developer) could adopt it bottom-up without IT approval?"

### path 4: look across complementary offerings
what happens before, during, and after using your product? what pain exists in the broader journey?

"the tool works great but onboarding takes 6 weeks. what if onboarding was the product?"

### path 5: look across functional-emotional appeal
some industries compete on function (price, specs). others on emotion (brand, design). what if you flipped it?

"everyone in B2B competes on features. what if you competed on the experience of using the product?"

### path 6: look across time
what trends are forming that will change what buyers value? position for where the market is going, not where it is.

"remote work isn't a trend — it's a permanent shift. what does that mean for [industry]?"

## the buyer utility map

a 6×6 matrix that systematically identifies where new value can be created.

**six stages of the buyer experience:**
1. Purchase — how easy is it to buy?
2. Delivery — how easy is it to get started?
3. Use — how easy is it to use daily?
4. Supplements — what else is needed to make it work?
5. Maintenance — how easy is it to maintain/update?
6. Disposal — how easy is it to switch away?

**six utility levers:**
1. Customer productivity
2. Simplicity
3. Convenience
4. Risk reduction
5. Fun and image
6. Environmental friendliness

→ map competitors on this grid. blank spaces = opportunity. everyone clusters in the same cells = red ocean on those dimensions.

## applying blue ocean to GTM

### positioning
don't position against competitors. position against the old way of doing things.

"we didn't build a better [category]. we asked: what if [category] worked completely differently?"

### pricing
blue ocean pricing follows "strategic pricing" — price to capture the mass of target buyers, not to maximize per-unit revenue. often means pricing lower than expected to accelerate adoption and lock out fast followers.

### messaging
lead with the factors you created or raised. don't mention eliminated factors — your non-customers don't care about them (that's the point).

### competitive response
when competitors enter your blue ocean (and they will), they'll try to compete on the factors you created. your defense: keep moving. create new factors. the value curve should keep evolving.

## deliverables when using this framework

1. **current value curve** — client vs. top 3 competitors across 8-12 factors
2. **ERRC grid** — eliminate, reduce, raise, create with specific factors
3. **new value curve** — the target state showing the blue ocean position
4. **six paths analysis** — which paths reveal the most promising opportunities
5. **strategic pricing recommendation** — price to capture mass, not maximize margin
6. **blue ocean messaging** — positioning that makes competition irrelevant
