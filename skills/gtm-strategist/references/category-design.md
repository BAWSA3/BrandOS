# Category Design Framework

based on Play Bigger (Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, Kevin Maney) and category strategy principles.

## core thesis

the most valuable companies don't win existing markets — they create new categories and become the "category king." category kings capture ~76% of the total value in their category. the rest fight over scraps.

this isn't just branding. it's a strategic choice to define the problem differently so you control the rules of competition.

## when to use category design

- the product is genuinely different — not "better CRM" but "a new way to think about customer relationships"
- competing in the existing category means fighting incumbents on their terms (their budgets, their distribution, their brand equity)
- the client has a strong, defensible point of view about how the world should work
- the market is ready for a new frame but nobody has named it yet

## when NOT to use it

- the product is an incremental improvement in an existing category (just position better)
- the client doesn't have a real POV — they just want to sound different
- the category is already well-defined and the client can win within it
- there's no budget or patience for the education phase (category creation takes 2-3 years to pay off)

## the category design process

### step 1: define the problem (not the solution)

category design starts with the problem, not the product. the goal is to name a problem that people recognize but haven't had a word for.

**exercise: the "before/after" frame**

```
BEFORE [category name]:
- [how people currently suffer with the old way]
- [specific frustration 1]
- [specific frustration 2]
- [what they're missing / blind spot]

AFTER [category name]:
- [how the world works with this new approach]
- [specific improvement 1]
- [specific improvement 2]
- [the shift in thinking / new normal]
```

→ if the before/after doesn't create a visceral "oh, that's what's been wrong" reaction, the category isn't sharp enough.

### step 2: name the category

the name should:
- be instantly understood (not jargon)
- imply the problem and the solution
- be ownable (not generic)
- work in a sentence: "[Company] is the leader in [category name]"

**naming patterns that work:**

| Pattern | Example |
|---------|---------|
| [Adjective] + [Existing noun] | "Conversational AI" |
| [New verb] + [Existing noun] | "Revenue Intelligence" (Gong) |
| [Compound noun] | "Customer Data Platform" (Segment) |
| [Reframe] | "Developer Experience" instead of "DevOps" |

**testing the name:**
- say it to 5 people outside the industry. do they get it in under 10 seconds?
- google it. is the term already owned by someone else?
- does it work as a job title? ("we're hiring a [category] leader")
- would an analyst write a market report about it?

### step 3: craft the POV (Point of View)

the POV is the intellectual backbone of the category. it's the argument for why the world needs this category to exist. it's not a mission statement — it's a manifesto.

**POV structure:**

```
1. THE SHIFT
   The world has changed in a fundamental way:
   [describe the macro shift — technology, behavior, regulation, economics]

2. THE PROBLEM
   This shift created a new problem that didn't exist before:
   [describe the problem that the old solutions can't address]

3. THE OLD WAY (why it fails)
   The current approach was built for a different era:
   [describe why incumbents/existing solutions are structurally unable to solve this]

4. THE NEW WAY (your category)
   [Category name] is a fundamentally different approach:
   [describe the new approach at a philosophical level, not feature level]

5. THE PROOF
   Companies that have adopted this approach are seeing:
   [data points, case studies, early results]

6. THE FUTURE
   In 3-5 years, every [type of company] will need [category]:
   [paint the picture of the inevitable future where this category is standard]
```

→ a strong POV makes people nod before you even mention your product. it's about the problem and the approach, not your features.

### step 4: plan the lightning strike

the lightning strike is a coordinated moment where you declare the category to the world. it's not a product launch — it's a category launch.

**lightning strike components:**

1. **category-defining content** — a report, manifesto, or book that establishes the intellectual framework (e.g., HubSpot's "Inbound Marketing" book)
2. **event or moment** — conference talk, virtual event, media blitz. concentrated attention.
3. **ecosystem activation** — analysts, partners, customers, and influencers all saying the same thing at the same time
4. **product announcement** — your product as the first (and best) example of the category in action
5. **PR / media** — not "Company X launches product" but "New category emerges to solve [problem]"

**timing:**
- 3-6 months of preparation before the strike
- all components launch within a 2-week window
- follow-up cadence for 90 days after (reinforcement, not repetition)

### step 5: the FrozenCat moment

from Play Bigger: the moment where you make the problem so vivid and visceral that people can't unsee it.

**what makes a good FrozenCat:**
- it's a specific, concrete scenario (not abstract)
- it triggers an emotional reaction (frustration, fear, embarrassment)
- everyone in the target audience has experienced it or can immediately relate
- it naturally leads to "there has to be a better way"

**examples:**
- Drift: "You fill out a form on a website and wait 3 days for a sales rep to call you back. In 2024. That's insane."
- Gong: "Your sales rep just had the most important call of the quarter and nobody knows what was said. The deal is in your CRM as 'looking good.'"

**exercise:**
"imagine your buyer's worst day at work — the moment where the old way fails spectacularly. describe it in 2 sentences. that's your FrozenCat."

## category king economics

why this matters strategically:

- category kings capture **76% of the market cap** in their category
- being #2 or #3 in a well-defined category is significantly less valuable than being #1 in a new one
- categories are self-reinforcing: analysts write about them, buyers budget for them, job titles reference them
- the company that names the category usually gets to define the criteria for evaluation (you set the rules competitors are judged by)

**the compounding effect:**
1. you name the category → 2. analysts cover it → 3. buyers create budget line items → 4. competitors validate the category by entering it → 5. you're the incumbent

## category design + GTM motion

| Client Stage | Category Design Application |
|-------------|---------------------------|
| Pre-revenue | focus on POV and FrozenCat. use to attract early believers, not mass market. |
| $1-10M ARR | lightning strike timing. you have enough proof points to be credible. |
| $10-50M ARR | analyst relations, partner ecosystem. institutionalize the category. |
| $50M+ ARR | defend category leadership. define subcategories before competitors do. |

## deliverables when using this framework

when you apply category design for a client, produce:

1. **category name** — tested, ownable, clear
2. **POV document** — the full 6-part manifesto
3. **FrozenCat** — the visceral problem statement
4. **competitive reframe** — how this positions incumbents as "the old way"
5. **lightning strike plan** — timing, components, budget allocation
6. **measurement framework** — how to track category awareness and ownership
